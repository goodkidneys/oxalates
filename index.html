Five pillars of stone prevention, ranked by evidence

  1. Fluids: 2.5–3.0 L/day so urine output is at least 2.5 L.
  2. Calcium with meals: 1,000–1,200 mg/day from food. It binds oxalate in the gut. Never skip it to "lower calcium".
  3. Sodium under 2,300 mg/day: high sodium raises urinary calcium.
  4. Moderate oxalate: drop only the very highest sources (spinach, chard, beet greens, rhubarb, almonds in quantity). Restrict further only with documented hyperoxaluria on a 24-hour urine.
  5. Plenty of fruit and vegetables: citrate, potassium, magnesium and phytate all protect.

Vegan diet and calcium oxalate kidney stones

Condensed from the evidence guide in this repository (PDF, 7 pages), with guideline references updated in September 2026.

The single most important finding

Eating enough calcium with meals matters more than avoiding oxalate. The Borghi randomized trial (NEJM 2002) showed a normal-calcium diet (1,200 mg/day) cut stone recurrence by 51% compared with a low-calcium diet, because dietary calcium binds oxalate in the gut before it reaches the kidneys. Oxalate still matters (spinach carries over 100 times the oxalate of kale per serving), but the primary focus is adequate calcium at every meal, not obsessive avoidance. A well-planned vegan diet can be protective: EPIC-Oxford found vegetarians had 31% lower stone risk than high meat eaters, and the DASH pattern cuts risk by 40–45%.

The 2023 Harvard update

Harvard T.H. Chan School of Public Health released a major update in November 2023, remeasuring hundreds of foods by liquid chromatography (Dr John Knight's lab, University of Alabama). Several results surprised clinicians:

  • Raspberries dropped to 2.5 mg per ½ cup. Pistachios fell from ~49 to 9 mg per oz, walnuts to 11.7 mg. Lentils (boiled) measured 2.4 mg per ½ cup. Arugula, measured for the first time, registered 0.3 mg per ½ cup. Ground flaxseed measured 0.2 mg per tbsp and green peas 0.5 mg per ½ cup.
  • The very-high foods stayed very high: cooked spinach 547 mg per ½ cup, Swiss chard 350–1,120 mg per cup, rhubarb 270–390 mg per ½ cup, beet greens 916 mg per ½ cup, beets 76 mg per ½ cup canned.
  • Some went up: blueberries 18.5 mg per ½ cup, edamame (green soybeans) 48 mg per ½ cup, tomato paste 24.5 mg per ¼ cup, bulgur 86 mg per cup, one whole baked potato with skin 92 mg.
  • Categories vary enormously. Soy products span from soft tofu (10.6 mg) to plant-based burgers (58 mg). Greens vary 100-fold: 250 mg of oxalate is half a cup of spinach or 125 cups of kale. Blanket avoidance of "greens", "soy" or "nuts" is not justified; the specific food matters.

Caveat: oxalate databases still disagree with each other. Penniston et al. found that switching between two reputable databases would reclassify about 40% of patients relative to their oxalate target. Content varies with cultivar, soil, season, ripeness and analytical method. The Harvard liquid-chromatography list is the most reliable current source; this site shows the Harvard row alongside every curated food so the disagreement is visible.

Five pillars, ranked by evidence strength

The AUA (2026), EAU (2026) and CARI (2026) guidelines converge on a multi-pronged dietary approach. None addresses vegan diets specifically, but the principles adapt.

  1. Fluid intake has the strongest evidence. AUA and EAU target urine output of at least 2.5 L/day, which usually means drinking 2.5–3.0 L. Water is primary. Coffee shows a protective association (about 19% lower risk per additional daily cup in a Mendelian randomization study). Citrus juices add citrate; a pragmatic RCT (Ruggenenti 2022) found daily lemon juice helps prevent recurrent stones, and a Dec 2025 Thai trial of a daily lime regimen reported 14% recurrence versus 45% over two years.
  2. Calcium 1,000–1,200 mg/day, from food, taken with meals. Dropping dietary calcium from ~1,000 to ~400 mg raises urinary oxalate by 28%. Supplements taken between meals raise stone risk by roughly 20%; if you need one, take calcium citrate with meals. This is the main vegan-specific challenge.
  3. Sodium under 2,300 mg/day (ideally under 2,000). Sodium and calcium share renal transport, so salt raises urinary calcium. Processed meat alternatives, canned goods and soy sauce are the vegan sodium traps.
  4. Moderate oxalate, not strict avoidance. A target of 40–100 mg/day applies to stone formers with documented hyperoxaluria (a typical diet contains 200–300 mg). CARI: "there is limited evidence that a low oxalate diet reduces the risk of calcium oxalate stones." Eliminate only the highest sources and pair moderate ones with calcium. A very restrictive diet can backfire by cutting potassium, magnesium, citrate, phytate and fiber.
  5. Abundant fruit and vegetables: citrate inhibits crystal formation, potassium alkalinizes urine, magnesium competes with calcium for oxalate binding, phytate inhibits crystallization. DASH reduced risk 40–45% in three cohorts totalling 240,000 people despite higher oxalate intake.

Plant calcium sources that beat dairy on absorption

Low-oxalate cruciferous vegetables have higher fractional calcium absorption than cow's milk (32%): bok choy 54%, broccoli 61%, turnip greens 52%, mustard greens 58%, kale 41%. The catch is serving size: a cup of cooked kale gives only 38–55 mg of absorbable calcium, so fortified foods are needed too.

  • Calcium-set tofu (calcium sulfate): 253–683 mg per ½ cup at ~30% absorption. The most efficient plant source.
  • Fortified plant milks: 300–450 mg per cup. Oat, soy, rice or coconut over almond (27 mg oxalate per cup) for stone formers. Shake the carton; the calcium settles.
  • Fortified orange juice: ~350 mg per cup plus citrate.
  • Low-oxalate greens: bok choy, kale, turnip greens, broccoli, mustard greens.
  • Calcium-fortified cereals and breads: often 100–300 mg per serving.

Spinach is the cautionary example: 245 mg calcium per cooked cup, but only about 5% is absorbed, so it yields 12 mg. Source selection matters, not just quantity.

The vegan paradox

Vegetarians show lower stone risk (EPIC-Oxford hazard ratio 0.69; Robertson 40–60% lower), and plant protein produces less acid load, more alkaline urine, higher citrate and lower urinary calcium. But no prospective study has examined strictly vegan diets, and the vegetarian benefit likely leans partly on dairy calcium. Ferraro et al. (2020) flagged vegan diets as a "theoretical risk" from lower calcium and higher oxalate exposure. The resolution is nutrient adequacy, not the label: a vegan diet with 1,000+ mg calcium from fortified foods and calcium-set tofu taken with meals, sodium under 2,300 mg, the very highest oxalate foods avoided and plenty of produce should be at least as protective as a mixed diet. A vegan diet built on spinach smoothies, almond milk and cashews with little fortified calcium can be strongly stone-forming.

Gut microbiome

Oxalobacter formigenes, the gut bacterium that lives on oxalate, is associated with about 70% lower recurrence, but only 30–40% of Western adults carry it. A 2025 colonization study (Kidney Int Rep) established it in 22 of 22 healthy adults with a single dose, cutting stool oxalate 54% and urinary oxalate 14%; a companion study (Gut Microbes 2025) showed that a person's baseline abundance of oxalate-degrading genes predicts whether they respond. Over 30% of gut species carry at least one oxalate-handling gene. Antibiotics matter: sulfa drugs raise stone odds by 133%, cephalosporins 88%, fluoroquinolones 67%, with risk highest 3–6 months after exposure. However, a December 2025 systematic review of nine studies concluded the evidence does not support probiotics for stone prevention, and no guideline recommends them. Practical takeaway: minimize unnecessary antibiotics and eat a diverse high-fiber plant diet; personalized probiotic therapy remains investigational.

Cooking methods that reduce oxalate

  • Boiling is the only method with consistently strong evidence: 30–87% reduction across vegetables (Chai and Liebman 2005), because soluble oxalate leaches into the water, which must be discarded. Small-chopped spinach boiled two minutes loses up to 72%; one study found boiling removed 87% versus 42% for steaming.
  • Soaking beans overnight before boiling roughly halves their oxalate. Sprouting reduces grain oxalate 24–48%.
  • Fermentation helps but varies: kimchi-style fermentation of chard cut total oxalate 38.5% over 5 days; sesame 69% over 12 days. Lactobacillus and Bifidobacterium degrade oxalate enzymatically.
  • Baking and roasting are ineffective and can concentrate oxalate by driving off water. Roasting nuts does not reduce their oxalate.
  • Best sequence: soak overnight, discard water, boil in fresh water, discard cooking water, eat with a calcium source.

Practical vegan food guide

  • Greens: arugula (0.3 mg), kale (~2 mg), bok choy (~1 mg), romaine (0.3 mg), watercress, mustard greens (~2 mg), turnip greens, cabbage (1.4 mg). Avoid spinach, Swiss chard, beet greens, sorrel, purslane.
  • Proteins: boiled lentils (2.4 mg per ½ cup), black beans (10.5 mg), red kidney beans (9.9 mg), chickpeas (~9 mg), black-eyed peas (3 mg), soft tofu (10.6 mg per 3.5 oz), veggie burgers (~5 mg), soy burgers (12 mg). Treat tempeh (23–48 mg), edamame (48 mg), pea-protein "plant-based" burgers (58 mg), navy beans (96 mg) and baked or refried beans (58–60 mg) as high. Soy milk is low (4–10 mg per cup; the old 336 mg figure was superseded). TVP and soy nuts are uncertain (10–58 mg per serving) and best kept small.
  • Nuts and seeds: pistachios (9 mg per oz), walnuts (11.7 mg), coconut (0.1 mg), pumpkin seeds (4.3 mg per ¼ cup), raw sunflower kernels (2.9 mg), ground flaxseed (0.2 mg per tbsp), pecans (15.7 mg). Limit peanuts (29 mg), cashews (63.5 mg), almonds (72–122 mg), almond butter (42 mg per tbsp), chia (uncertain, possibly ~55 mg per tbsp).
  • Grains: white rice, oats, oat bran (0 mg), barley (0 mg), corn flakes, regular pasta (16.5 mg per cup), rye or oat bread. Limit quinoa (54 mg per cup), whole wheat pasta (46 mg), bulgur (86 mg), wheat berries (98 mg), buckwheat (133 mg), bran cereals (35–46 mg), amaranth (~1,510 mg per 100 g dry).
  • Fruits: pomegranate (0.3 mg), cranberries (0.3 mg), raspberries (2.5 mg), strawberries (1.2 mg), grapes, melon, cherries, peaches (0.4 mg), pears (1.8 mg), apples (0.7 mg), pineapple (5.2 mg), bananas (10 mg). Oranges (23 mg) and grapefruit (13 mg) bring citrate that offsets their oxalate. Blueberries are moderate (18.5 mg). Avoid starfruit (very high and toxic in kidney disease).
  • Beverages: water first; coffee (1.7 mg); lemon water (4 oz lemon juice daily raises urinary citrate); fortified oat, soy, rice or coconut milk; calcium-fortified orange juice. Tea is 6.4 mg per cup, but matcha is roughly 34 mg per 2 g serving. Limit almond milk (27 mg), cashew milk, carrot juice (28 mg), hot chocolate (65 mg) and lemonade from concentrate (22 mg per 12 oz).
  • Supplements: avoid vitamin C above 1,000 mg/day (a July 2026 meta-analysis: supplements raise urinary oxalate by about 10 mg per day). Turmeric supplements measure 8.6 mg oxalate per dose, mostly soluble; cinnamon 14 mg per dose, mostly insoluble (Thaker 2026, Knight lab).

Conclusion

The evidence supports a whole-diet approach rather than obsessive oxalate avoidance. Three actions matter most: 1,000–1,200 mg calcium daily from fortified foods and low-oxalate sources, timed with meals; 2.5–3.0 L of fluid daily; sodium under 2,300 mg. Eliminate only the handful of extremely high-oxalate foods while keeping the diet's advantages: alkaline urine, high citrate, high potassium, abundant phytate and fiber. Most importantly, a 24-hour urine collection is the only way to know whether urinary oxalate is actually driving your stones. Many calcium oxalate stone formers have high urinary calcium, not high oxalate, which makes calcium-focused rather than oxalate-focused intervention even more important.

Guideline references (checked Sept 2026)

  • AUA, Medical Management of Kidney Stones, Parts I and II, J Urol, online 30 July 2026 (replaces 2014/2019): dietary calcium 1,000–1,200 mg/day primarily with meals; moderate high-oxalate foods for CaOx stones with hyperoxaluria; stricter restriction plus meal-timed calcium for enteric hyperoxaluria.
  • EAU Urolithiasis Guidelines 2026: fluid 2.5–3 L; normal calcium (minimum 800, general 1,000 mg/day); oxalate restriction if hyperoxaluria (weak); avoid excessive vitamin C.
  • CARI 2026 (Kidney Int Rep): nutrition therapy central; citrate reduces recurrence but is poorly tolerated.
  • NKF "Kidney Stone Diet and Prevention Tips" (updated July 2026): strict low-oxalate diet "not usually the best approach"; individualize on 24-hour urine.
  • Cleveland Clinic kidney stone diet page (March 2026): keep calcium, pair it with oxalate-containing foods.

Second source, used where Harvard has no row (many vegetables, potatoes, tempeh). Largely the same Knight/UAB measurements published through a different channel, so the two lists are not independent.

Nutrition per 100 g comes from USDA FoodData Central (fdc.nal.usda.gov) and is approximate. USDA does not publish oxalate.

Risk levels used on this site

Per listed serving: Low 0–10 mg, Moderate 11–25 mg, High 26–99 mg, Very high 100 mg and over. Recipes use per-serving thresholds of 10 / 25 / 50 mg. A disputed tag means published sources disagree or no assay exists; the number shown is the most defensible one and the notes explain the range. A star (★) marks foods whose classification changed with newer data.

How to update this site

Add a recipe
  1. Open Recipe Builder, pick ingredients from the database, enter amounts. Oxalate and nutrition totals compute as you go.
  2. Either press Publish to GitHub (after saving a GitHub token once in the publisher settings on that page; the token stays in this browser only), or press Copy JSON and paste it into a Claude Code session with "add this recipe", or paste it into data/recipes.js on GitHub's web editor.
  3. GitHub Pages redeploys within a minute or two.
Add or correct a food, or re-check the data online

Start a Claude Code session on the repository and ask, for example: "Add fresh figs to the curated food list with the Harvard value", or "Check whether Harvard or OHF have published a newer oxalate table and update the data." The repository's CLAUDE.md tells the session where everything lives and what format to keep; tools/validate.js checks the data files before a push.

If Harvard publishes a new spreadsheet: drop it into docs/, run python3 tools/harvard_xlsx_to_js.py docs/NEW-FILE.xlsx, review the diff, and commit.

Privacy of this site

The page carries noindex, nofollow, noarchive robots tags and the site serves a robots.txt that disallows all crawlers, so search engines will not index it. It has no links from anywhere. Anyone who has the URL can open it; there is no login. The GitHub repository behind it is public (GitHub Pages on the free plan requires that); making it private needs a paid GitHub plan.
